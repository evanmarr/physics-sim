Scientist: Emmy Noether

Emmy Noether (1882-1935) was a German mathematician whose 1915 theorem connects two ideas that seem unrelated at first: symmetry and conservation. She proved that every continuous symmetry of a physical system's action corresponds to a conserved quantity — a result so foundational that Einstein called her a 'creative mathematical genius' in a letter to the New York Times.

Noether did this work while unpaid and officially unable to hold a professorship at Gottingen because she was a woman; David Hilbert reportedly pushed back on the faculty's objections by asking, 'I do not see that the sex of the candidate is against her admission. After all, the university senate is not a bathhouse.'

Equation: Noether's Theorem (informal statement)

Symmetry in time -> conservation of energy. Symmetry in space -> conservation of momentum. Rotational symmetry -> conservation of angular momentum.

This isn't one equation so much as a recipe for finding conservation laws: look for what stays the same when you shift a system in some way, and Noether's theorem hands you a quantity that's conserved. Energy conservation, for instance, falls directly out of the fact that the laws of physics don't change from one moment to the next.

Fact of the Day: A day on Venus is longer than its year

Venus rotates so slowly — once every 243 Earth days — that it takes longer to spin once on its axis than it does to complete a full orbit around the Sun (225 Earth days). To make it stranger, Venus rotates backwards relative to most planets, so on Venus the Sun rises in the west.
